AML Alert Prioritization

Ranking automated anti-money-laundering alerts by the probability a human compliance officer would escalate them — so reviewers spend time where it counts.

01

The problem

Financial institutions generate thousands of automated AML alerts a day. Manually reviewing every one is slow, and most turn out to be nothing — a compliance officer dismisses roughly 5 out of every 6. Our goal is a model that reads a customer's transaction history behind each alert and scores the likelihood a human would escalate it, so review queues can be sorted by risk instead of by arrival time.

02

Data structure

Two relational sources are joined per alert: a static signals table (one row per alert) and a transaction log (many rows per alert) holding each customer's activity behind it.

Exploratory analysis

Two things shaped how we modeled this: the target is heavily imbalanced, and risk shows up more in how money moves than in how much.

04

Our approach

We aggregate each customer's transaction log into per-alert features: transaction count and total amount, plus the direction (incoming vs. outgoing) and channel mix (cash, card, domestic transfer, international) behind Figure 2 — roughly 19 features in total. Models were trained on an 80/20 split and compared on validation ROC-AUC.

LightGBM 0.5516
Logistic Regression 0.5467
Random Forest 0.5399
CONCLUSION Adding the channel and direction breakdown lifted all three models from ~0.51 to the 0.54–0.55 range, confirming the behavioral features carry real signal. LightGBM now leads, likely because its tree splits can capture interactions between features (e.g. cash share combined with transaction volume) that a linear model can't express on its own — so it's our current submission model.
